**If you like *True West*, the Japanese plays most worth reading next are the ones that understand two brothers trapped in the same room are never really two people — they are one failed self arguing with itself.**

Sam Shepard's *True West* premiered at the Magic Theatre in San Francisco on July 10, 1980. Austin, the polite screenwriter house-sitting at his mother's place in the California desert, is working on a Hollywood screenplay. Lee, his older brother — a drifter, a thief, a man who lives in the desert and steals television sets — walks in and will not leave. Over the course of nine scenes, their roles reverse. The screenwriter picks up a nine-iron and starts swinging. The thief tries to type a Western. The kitchen fills with stolen toasters. The curtain falls on two brothers facing each other with their fists raised, lights fading, nothing resolved.

Shepard said the play was about "double nature." Lee and Austin are not two characters so much as two halves of a single American psyche — the civilized self and the feral self, the writer and the outlaw, the man who believes in work and the man who believes in nothing. The frontier is gone. The myth of the West is a studio pitch. And the two brothers cannot find a way to stop being each other.

That exact structure — **a self at war with itself, dressed up as a family at war with itself** — turns out to be a core preoccupation of modern Japanese drama. Postwar Japanese playwrights, from the angura (underground) generation onward, keep returning to the same questions Shepard was asking: What happens when the old myth of masculinity collapses? What happens when a brother shows up and will not leave? What happens when the person you are trying to escape is wearing your face?

For English-speaking readers, directors, dramaturgs, and theater programs, the five Japanese plays below are not scene-by-scene copies of *True West*. They are useful because they continue Shepard's argument in a different cultural key: **the self you cannot outrun is usually sitting across the kitchen table.**

## Why *True West* Opens the Door to Japanese Drama

*True West* is American on the surface — the Mojave, the coyotes, Kirk Douglas, the Hollywood pitch meeting. Peel one layer back and it is something older. It is a play about **inheritance that will not stay inherited**. The father, offstage, is a drunk living in the desert, abandoned by both sons. The mother is away in Alaska. The sons return to an empty house that is not really theirs, and in the vacuum of parents they turn into each other.

Japanese modern drama has been circling the same pattern for over sixty years. Shingeki (新劇, new drama) imported Western realism in the early twentieth century partly to tell stories about family duty, inheritance, and the pressure to become a particular kind of man. The angura (アングラ) generation of the 1960s — Shuji Terayama, Juro Kara, Minoru Betsuyaku — blew up that family box from inside, filling it with wrestlers, prostitutes, ghosts, and desert wanderers. Contemporary playwrights inherited both impulses. The result is a long shelf of Japanese plays where **a brother arrives, a role reverses, a kitchen becomes a battlefield, and the question "which of us is really the man of this house?" has no answer that anyone wants.**

The five plays below take that inheritance in five different directions:

- a two-brother factory nightmare where "the strong one" and "the weak one" trade places,
- an aging family play about a prodigal older brother who walks back in after decades,
- an ensemble piece about seven siblings forced into one room by a dying father who killed their mother,
- a raw Zainichi Korean family drama where the father's violence is the inheritance,
- and a 1980s cult classic about a Japanese family literally adrift from itself.

Each one offers a different way to continue the conversation Shepard started.

## 1. *Machine Diary* (Machine Nikki, マシーン日記) — Suzuki Matsuo (松尾スズキ)

- **Japanese Play Library:** [Machine Diary](https://gikyokutosyokan.com/posts/205)
- **Cast:** 2 men, 2 women (4 actors)
- **Runtime:** about 120 minutes
- **Best for *True West* fans who love:** the two-brother pressure cooker, role reversal, the moment civility snaps, and comedy that goes somewhere very dark

If the thing that stays with you after *True West* is the scene where Austin finally picks up the golf club, or the moment you realize the "weak" brother has been the dangerous one all along, this is the Japanese play to read next.

Suzuki Matsuo (松尾スズキ) is the founder of Otona Keikaku (大人計画), one of the most influential small-theater companies of the 1990s and 2000s, and *Machine Diary* is one of his signature works. First staged in 1996 and revived repeatedly since — including a celebrated 2001 production at Honda Theatre in Shimokitazawa — it reads like what *True West* might have looked like if Shepard had written it inside a prefab shed next to a factory in a Japanese industrial town.

The setup is almost unbearably economical. Akitoshi (アキトシ), the older brother, runs a small factory. Michio (ミチオ), the younger brother, lives in a prefab shed next to it. Michio is literally chained up. He raped a woman named Sachiko who worked at the factory; Akitoshi took responsibility by marrying her. Into this already toxic domestic machinery walks Keiko, Sachiko's former middle school P.E. teacher, who decides — rationally, systematically — that Michio's total worthlessness is attractive to her. She tells him to call her "Machine Number 3." As long as they are having sex, she will do anything he says.

What follows is the Shepard engine, but ratcheted into Japanese small-theater grotesque: the "strong" brother managing everything, the "weak" brother chained up in the shed, and then — exactly as in *True West* — the floor gives way. Power shifts. Dignity shifts. The one holding the leash becomes the one on the leash. Comedy becomes horror becomes something sadder than either.

Suzuki Matsuo's language is pitched between crude slang and sudden lyricism; the laughs are enormous and immediately followed by an airlessness. For English-speaking directors interested in Shepard's two-hander logic blown up into a four-hander, there is almost nothing in the Japanese canon that fits this slot better.

**Why it rhymes with *True West*:** two brothers who have divided one personality between them, a confined domestic space the characters cannot leave, a role reversal that erupts out of comedy into violence, and a final image that offers no exit.

## 2. *Brother Returns* (Ani Kaeru, 兄帰る) — Ai Nagai (永井愛)

- **Japanese Play Library:** [Brother Returns](https://gikyokutosyokan.com/posts/50)
- **Cast:** 4 men, 4 women (8 actors)
- **Runtime:** about 150 minutes
- **Best for *True West* fans who love:** the prodigal-brother arrival scene, the panic a family feels when the "wrong" sibling comes home, and the comedy of a disruption nobody can politely refuse

If what hooks you about *True West* is the first fifteen minutes — the moment Lee walks in, drops a beer on the counter, and the whole family geometry warps around him — this is the Japanese play to read next.

Ai Nagai (永井愛) is one of the most important women playwrights in contemporary Japan and the co-founder of the company Nitosha (二兎社). Her work is often described as social realism with a comic engine, and she has won the Kishida Kunio Drama Prize and the Tsuruya Nanboku Memorial Prize, among many others. *Brother Returns* (1999) is one of her most beloved plays and a very frequently revived piece in the Japanese repertory.

The premise is quiet and devastating. A married middle-class couple is going through a difficult period. Into their home arrives the wife's older brother — a drifter, broke, unmoored, carrying nothing but trouble — along with a cast of connected relatives and friends who orbit the family. The brother is not evil. He is simply the kind of man a careful family tries not to become. His physical presence in the house turns every quiet failure of the couple's marriage into an audible one.

Where Shepard reaches straight for mythic violence, Nagai reaches for the slow burn of middle-class embarrassment. The question is the same question *True West* asks under its toaster-stealing surface: **what do you do when the family member you tried to leave behind walks back into the room and refuses to admit that he has been left behind?** The couple cannot kick him out. They cannot absorb him. They cannot stop watching him. Over 150 minutes, every one of them has to decide which version of themselves they are willing to be with him in the room.

For small- and mid-size English-speaking theaters, *Brother Returns* is a particularly practical choice: eight-actor cast, strong roles for women (unusual for a brother-centered play), a kitchen-sink setting that works on almost any stage, and a tone that lands as comedy right until it does not.

**Why it rhymes with *True West*:** the prodigal brother who disrupts a settled household, the question of which sibling is "really" the stable one, and the way a kitchen becomes the moral courtroom of a family.

## 3. *Wilderness 1/7* (Kōya 1/7, 荒野1/7) — Noboru Takagi (高木登)

- **Japanese Play Library:** [Wilderness 1/7](https://gikyokutosyokan.com/posts/121)
- **Cast:** 4 men, 3 women (7 actors)
- **Runtime:** about 80 minutes
- **Best for *True West* fans who love:** the desert as an idea more than a place, inherited violence, and siblings who do not actually know each other anymore

The title alone will catch any *True West* reader. *Kōya* (荒野) means "wilderness" or "wasteland" — the same imaginative territory Shepard's Mojave occupies — and "1/7" means "one of seven." Noboru Takagi's play (presented at the Japan Play Library) uses the figure of the wilderness not as geography but as a condition of family.

The premise is severe. Years ago, a father killed his mother. The seven children were separated, raised apart, and have had little real contact since. Now the eldest brother has summoned them back. The father is dying. The hospital wants a family decision about whether to continue life-sustaining treatment. Some of the siblings barely remember what happened. Some remember too much. As they talk, the parents come back into focus — not as the story anyone was told but as whoever they really were. The question that detonates in the room is bigger than the medical one: **why did he do it, and what does it mean that we are his seven?**

This is the play on this list that is most structurally similar to American family drama of the Shepard–Albee–O'Neill line, and most explicitly engaged with the idea that **a family's violence is not something that happened once; it is something that distributed itself across seven lives.** Where *True West* compresses that distribution into two brothers and a nine-iron, *Kōya 1/7* lets it fan out into seven people trying to agree on a single memory.

For English-speaking theater makers interested in a Japanese counterpart to American "buried past comes home" plays, this is one of the strongest fits currently represented on the Japanese Play Library.

**Why it rhymes with *True West*:** the wilderness as metaphor, violence inherited from a father who is both absent and overwhelming, and siblings reckoning with the question of what — if anything — they owe one another.

## 4. *Fish Festival* (Sakana no Matsuri, 魚の祭) — Yu Miri (柳美里)

- **Japanese Play Library:** [Fish Festival](https://gikyokutosyokan.com/posts/760)
- **Author:** Yu Miri (柳美里), b. 1968
- **Awards:** 37th Kishida Kunio Drama Prize (1993), won jointly with Akio Miyazawa — making Yu, at 24, the youngest Kishida winner at that time
- **Best for *True West* fans who love:** raw family violence, identity as inheritance, and language that refuses to let anyone hide

If what makes *True West* unforgettable for you is the sheer physical mess — the toasters, the plant-pots, the kitchen floor — handed to you as a theatrical argument about identity, this is the Japanese play to seek out next.

Yu Miri (柳美里) is one of the most consequential Zainichi Korean writers in Japan. She trained as an actor in the Tokyo Kid Brothers theater company and launched her own unit, Seishun Gogatsu-tō (青春五月党), in 1988. *Fish Festival* (魚の祭), performed in 1993, won her the Kishida Kunio Drama Prize — the top award for new drama in Japan — at the age of 24, co-winner with Akio Miyazawa. The play is one of her early signatures and remains one of the most startling family dramas of the 1990s.

*Fish Festival* works a territory that *True West* only hints at from inside Austin and Lee's drunk Hollywood scene: **identity as a wound passed down the family line.** The family at the center of the play is Zainichi — ethnic Koreans born and raised in Japan — and the pressure of that layered identity presses against ordinary family dysfunction until the two become indistinguishable. Memory, nationhood, and the body itself become sites of conflict. The violence is not comic. It is close in temperature to Shepard's late-play violence: real, embarrassed, and impossible to leave the room with.

For readers coming to Japanese drama through *True West*, this is the play that most clearly answers the implicit American question — *what if the frontier was not a desert but an identity you were born into?* — in a specifically Japanese register.

**Why it rhymes with *True West*:** violence that is both inherited and intimate, identity as the real battlefield, and a theatrical language that refuses to be polite about any of it.

## 5. *Drifting Family* (Hyōryū Kazoku, 漂流家族) — Tetsu Yamazaki (山崎哲)

- **Japanese Play Library:** [Drifting Family](https://gikyokutosyokan.com/posts/752)
- **Author:** Tetsu Yamazaki (山崎哲), b. 1946
- **Awards:** Kishida Kunio Drama Prize winner
- **Best for *True West* fans who love:** the collapse of the home as a shared concept, 1980s hard-edged domestic drama, and a title that is itself the thesis

If what you want after *True West* is another play from 1980s-adjacent new drama that treats the family unit as a thing coming apart in real time — one more look at what Shepard called "double nature" when it has gone pluralized — this is the Japanese play to find.

Tetsu Yamazaki (山崎哲) came up through the angura and post-angura generation and became one of the key voices of Japanese drama's shift from mythic to documentary-adjacent family writing in the late 1970s and 1980s. He often based his plays on real criminal cases, interrogating what in ordinary families mutates into catastrophe. *Drifting Family* is one of his most important works and won him the Kishida Kunio Drama Prize.

The play's title is doing enormous work. *Hyōryū* (漂流) is the word used for shipwrecked sailors and lost drift-boats — people and vessels pulled by currents, no longer in control of their course. To call a family *hyōryū* in Japanese is to say that the thing that is supposed to be the most anchored unit of life has become its most unmoored. The play follows that premise through every corner of its ensemble, dismantling the notion of the modern Japanese family not through one spectacular violence but through a series of small, cumulative losses of footing.

If *True West* is a play about two men who cannot find the frontier anymore, *Drifting Family* is a play about a whole family that cannot find the harbor anymore. The two share, in different keys, the recognition that **the old container has stopped containing anything.**

**Why it rhymes with *True West*:** the family as a myth that is failing in real time, the collapse of a stabilizing idea (the frontier for Shepard, the Japanese household for Yamazaki), and a theatrical honesty about the fact that no one in the room has an exit strategy.

## How to Program These Plays Together

If you are a director, academic, or theater company looking at *True West* and asking which Japanese play to put beside it in a season, in a syllabus, or in a staged reading series, here are the quickest practical pairings:

| If you want to pair with *True West* for… | Choose |
|---|---|
| A two-actor-style dark-comedy showcase | *Machine Diary* (松尾スズキ) |
| A season about "the prodigal returns" | *Brother Returns* (永井愛) |
| A comparative-literature unit on inherited violence | *Wilderness 1/7* (高木登) |
| An academic conversation on identity, race, and family in modern drama | *Fish Festival* (柳美里) |
| A survey of 1980s family drama across cultures | *Drifting Family* (山崎哲) |

For a Japanese-plays-in-translation reading series oriented toward *True West* fans, a strong three-play arc would be *Machine Diary* → *Brother Returns* → *Wilderness 1/7*: two brothers in one room, then a brother disrupting a settled house, then seven siblings trying to reconstruct what a brother or sister even is after a parent's violence.

For a longer unit — six nights, or a semester — add *Fish Festival* and *Drifting Family* at the end as the two plays that step back and ask the larger questions *True West* only gestures at: identity as inheritance, and the family itself as a form that may no longer hold.

## Reading Order for Someone New to Japanese Drama

If you are new to Japanese theater and are using *True West* as your doorway, this is a workable reading order:

1. **Start with *Brother Returns* (兄帰る, Ai Nagai).** It is the most accessible, has strong parts for women, and lands easily on English-reading audiences.
2. **Then read *Machine Diary* (マシーン日記, Suzuki Matsuo).** This is the one that will feel most immediately like a Japanese answer to Shepard. Fair warning: it goes to very dark places with its two brothers.
3. **Then *Wilderness 1/7* (荒野1/7, Noboru Takagi)** for the ensemble-scale version of inherited violence.
4. **Then *Fish Festival* (魚の祭, Yu Miri)** for the identity-as-wound dimension that *True West* only hints at.
5. **Finish with *Drifting Family* (漂流家族, Tetsu Yamazaki)** for the broadest, most "state of the Japanese family" reading, and the one that most clearly historicizes the others.

## FAQ

**Q: Is there a Japanese *True West* in the sense of a famous two-brother, kitchen-set two-hander?**
The closest single answer is *Machine Diary* (マシーン日記) by Suzuki Matsuo. It is a four-hander rather than a two-hander, but its central engine is a pair of brothers trading places inside a confined domestic space, and it is the Japanese small-theater work most often compared to this kind of American drama.

**Q: Are any of these plays available in English translation?**
Translation availability varies. Yu Miri is widely translated in prose (including her novel *Tokyo Ueno Station*, winner of the US National Book Award for Translated Literature in 2020), but her plays are less widely available. Ai Nagai has had some English-language stagings. For current rights and translation status, writers and theaters should contact the authors' representing agencies or the publishers listed on each play's Japanese Play Library page.

**Q: Which of these plays would work for a university theater program?**
*Brother Returns* and *Wilderness 1/7* are the most immediately workable. Both offer balanced casts and non-gratuitous emotional stakes. *Machine Diary* is extraordinary but contains sexual violence and content that requires serious dramaturgical care in a student context.

**Q: Which has the smallest cast?**
*Machine Diary* with 4 actors (2 men, 2 women). All five plays are listed with full cast information on the Japanese Play Library.

**Q: Where can I read more about Japanese playwrights adjacent to this list?**
See the Japanese Play Library's longer profile pieces, including [Playwright Spotlight: Yukio Mishima](https://gikyokutosyokan.com/blog/en/guide-playwright-yukio-mishima-2026) and [Playwright Spotlight: Shuji Terayama](https://gikyokutosyokan.com/blog/en/guide-playwright-shuji-terayama-2026).

## The Deeper Argument

What *True West* finally argues — and what makes it durable long after the Hollywood pitch jokes have aged — is that **the self is a myth and so is the family, and both of them keep going anyway.** Austin and Lee cannot stop being each other. The desert will not come back. The father will not come back. The mother returns, briefly, and then leaves again. There is no frontier. The brothers remain.

The five Japanese plays above continue that argument in a different theatrical tradition. They say, in their own vocabularies: the brother remains. The sister remains. The father's violence remains. The identity remains. The drifting family remains. The myth collapses, and the people inside it have to keep sitting across from each other anyway.

For readers coming to Japanese drama through Sam Shepard, that recognition — that the kitchen table is the stage on which all the big arguments still happen — is the shared ground. Everything else is dialect.
